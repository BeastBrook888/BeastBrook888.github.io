August 2026 fixed-effects regression · instrumental variables

Does five stars’ worth of usage fit on one team?

~/nba_star_fit · hand-rolled weighted least squares in NumPy

The question
A lineup only has 100 usage points to share. If you assemble five players who have each built a career on carrying an offense, whose share has to give — and does anyone actually get more efficient when asked to do less?
Method
Two estimators per player, both within-player by construction. First, minutes-weighted means in games with a co-star available against games without, with a 4,000-replication bootstrap for the interval. Second, a minutes-weighted least-squares regression of the outcome on a 0/1 availability dummy for every co-star simultaneously, plus season fixed effects and a home dummy, with heteroskedasticity-robust HC1 standard errors computed by hand. A third pass instruments usage with co-star availability in a two-stage least-squares estimate of the efficiency-versus-usage slope. No statsmodels and no scikit-learn — the normal equations, the robust sandwich and the bootstrap are all written out.
Data
7,857 player-game rows across 20 players and 44 team-seasons, 2010 through 2026, parsed from Basketball-Reference and held in a permanent local cache so the analysis re-runs offline.
What I found
  1. The cost of a co-star is large and precisely estimated for some players: Jaylen Brown’s usage rises +8.18 points when Jayson Tatum sits (95% CI +6.75 to +9.61); Tyrese Maxey’s rises +6.45 without Joel Embiid.
  2. And essentially zero for others. Embiid’s own usage moves +0.20 points when Maxey sits (CI −2.05 to +2.41) — he is already at his ceiling regardless of who else is on the floor.
  3. Maxey is the one player whose efficiency slope excludes zero: −0.68 true-shooting points per extra point of usage (CI −1.44 to −0.02). He gets better with less.
  4. The binding constraint turns out to be arithmetic, not efficiency. Five established workloads sum to 147 usage points against a budget of 100, and even at each player’s most-crowded observed rate they still sum to 132.5 — a 32.5-point overhang that no efficiency gain closes. The answer is staggering minutes, not sharing the ball.
What I’d do differently

Three real problems. The standard errors are robust but not clustered, and absences come in multi-week spells, so the intervals above are narrower than they should be. I report roughly 147 nominal-95% intervals across all players and outcomes with no multiple-comparison correction, then promote the largest to the headline — which is exactly how a false positive gets a table row. And a typo in one player ID silently returned a Basketball-Reference index page instead of a game log; because the file was large enough to look valid to my cache check, that co-star simply vanished from the model with no error raised. That one is a good argument for asserting row counts after every parse.

July 2026 Markov model · Monte Carlo · backtest

Will he play tonight? — an availability model

~/nba-availability-model · pure standard-library Python

Why I built it
To answer the criticism of my own article. Six weeks after publishing the Bridges piece, I went back and named the four things the LSTM got wrong — it gave a never-injured player near-zero risk, treated each game as independent when absences arrive in multi-week blocks, never compounded per-game risk into the season-long question actually being asked, and had no real out-of-sample test. This rebuilds it to fix all four.
Method
A two-state Markov model: available → unavailable at a per-game onset hazard, then unavailable for a duration drawn from that player’s own observed absence lengths. The hazard is partially pooled toward a league prior with empirical Bayes (method-of-moments Beta, α = 2.71, β = 29.80), which is what stops a one-absence player from being assigned impossible durability. Each player’s season is then simulated 40,000 times. Two parameters per player, no covariates — rest days are computed and displayed but deliberately kept out of the fit.
Validation
A genuine temporal holdout: fit on 2021–22 through 2024–25, tested on 2025–26, 1,311 held-out player-games, scored against deliberately dumb baselines on Brier score and log loss, with a reliability table and interval coverage.
What I found
  1. Mikal Bridges missed 1 of 325 at-risk games in training. Pooled, that is a 1.04% per-game hazard — which compounded across 82 games makes a perfect season a 41.2% proposition. The streak was closer to a coin flip than to a certainty.
  2. Clustering carries almost all the short-horizon signal: one-step-ahead Brier 0.136 against 0.268 for the same player’s marginal rate. Knowing whether he played last night matters more than knowing who he is.
  3. Preseason, player identity carries almost nothing — the pooled model (0.268) barely separates from a flat league base rate (0.270). Worth reporting precisely because it is the unflattering result.
  4. The 90% predictive intervals only covered 68.8% of realised season totals. They are too narrow, and the write-up says so.
What I’d do differently

Absence durations are redrawn independently at each onset, so the model returns injured players too soon — that shows up plainly as the worst-calibrated bin in the reliability table. The 16-player cohort was hand-picked to span durable and fragile archetypes, which means the pooling prior describes that cohort rather than the league. And the simulation propagates season-to-season randomness but not uncertainty in the fitted parameters, which is most of why the intervals are too narrow.
