Can five ball-dominant stars share a floor — and who defends when they do?

~/nba_star_fit · numpy and pandas only; the estimators are written out

The question
Philadelphia’s 2026-27 roster puts Tyrese Maxey, Joel Embiid, LeBron James, Jaylen Brown and rookie VJ Edgecombe on one team. A lineup has a finite amount of ball to go round and five men who have each built a career on holding it. Whose share gives — and once you have answered that, who guards anybody?
Method
Four independent readings, because no single one is trustworthy. Within-player usage elasticities from co-star absences, with season fixed effects, hand-computed HC1 errors and a 4,000-replication bootstrap; a two-stage least-squares pass instruments usage with those absences to get the efficiency-versus-usage slope. Matchup-level defence against leave-one-out baselines, residualised for shot mix after the raw version turned out to correlate −0.73 with the share of threes a defender faces. On-court lineups rebuilt from play-by-play substitutions for 27 seasons — validated against independent box scores to 0.43 minutes per game on average, 0.75 at worst — feeding a ridge-regularised adjusted plus-minus and a proper on/off that can hold a teammate fixed. And tracking data for what usage was only ever a proxy for — every metric put through a year-over-year reliability test before it was allowed to carry a conclusion, which is how one defensive measure came to be dropped at r = +0.10 and how rim FG% allowed earned its place at +0.66, above the matchup metric this study had been treating as primary. And a comparison against the 25 teams since 2000 that assembled three-plus high-usage players, at least two newly acquired — scored on wins minus the preseason Vegas line, because the line already prices the talent, with each star's input taken from the seasons before he arrived so the predictor stays out of the outcome year. No statsmodels, no scikit-learn: the normal equations, the robust sandwich, the ridge and the bootstrap are all in the source.
Data
27 seasons of play-by-play rebuilt into 1.3 million lineup stints; 2.0 million matchup rows across nine seasons plus nine more of playoffs; 8,185 player-games from Basketball-Reference; and 141 tracking and play-type files reaching back to 2013-14, regular season and playoffs. Roughly 3 GB, cached locally so every figure re-runs offline.
What I found
  1. The measurement I spent the project without turned the headline around. Usage says Embiid is the second-largest claim on the ball. Time of possession says he is the smallest of the five — 3.8 minutes a game at 3.38 seconds per touch, below the 3.60 average among rotation players. He runs no pick-and-roll as ball-handler and 22% post-ups, and a post-up is a short possession. Enormous usage, minimal ball-holding. It is not a one-year artifact: ball-holding correlates +0.86 to +0.93 from season to season, and across all three seasons of tracking data Embiid is last of the group every year and Maxey first every year.
  2. So the crowding is real but lands elsewhere. Five players at 36 minutes hold 180 of a game’s 240 player-minutes, so they are entitled to three-quarters of their team’s possession time. These five want 154% of that — an overhang of 9.7 minutes a game — and held to the four who played all three seasons it reads 160%, 162%, 156%, so it is a property of the players rather than of one roster. The man who has to give something up is Maxey (8.0 minutes per 36, 5.79 seconds per touch), not Embiid.
  3. Deterrence, finally measured. The project spent months unable to test “his presence discouraged the shot”. Playoff tracking answers it: opponents attack Embiid at the rim 1.34 fewer times per 36 in the postseason against a league-wide shift of −0.06 — 3.7 standard errors out, with every other player in the group indistinguishable from the league. It agrees with the regular-season on/off shot profile, from a separate dataset, which shows opponent rim rate falling 2.0 points with him on the floor. The rim percentage in the playoffs is unreadable by contrast — 21 to 70 attempts gives a ±6 to ±11 point noise floor — so I report the volume and not the rate.
  4. Embiid is the best defender of the five on five independent methods — matchup data, Basketball-Reference on/off, my own segment on/off, a pooled adjusted plus-minus, and now player tracking, where he defends rim attempts at a 95th-percentile rate and still holds them to 57.4% against a league mark of 64.9%. He is also the only one of the five who improves in the playoffs (−2.41 points of opponent shooting against his regular-season mark, on a league-wide shift of −0.06). Maxey is the counterweight: 0th percentile for rim-defence volume in all three seasons.
  5. Maxey and Edgecombe want the same shots: 0.989 cosine similarity across 16 court zones, more alike than 99% of league pairs. The backcourt of the future is the sharpest fit problem in the shot data.
  6. The ball does not concentrate in the playoffs — which is the opposite of the usual claim, and it matters because this roster exists to win playoff games. Across 12 postseasons and 1,245 player-postseasons, the same man holds the ball −0.145 minutes per 36 less [−0.179, −0.110], and the top quartile of regular-season ball-holders moves −0.192 against the bottom quartile’s −0.107. Embiid holds it even less when it matters (−0.47 over seven postseasons). Maxey is the exception: 7.96 to 10.16 in 2025-26, on 11 games.
  7. Set against the 25 comparable teams, it is not how much ball they collectively want that matters. Combined demand correlates +0.04 with beating the line — the hungriest group of the tracking era, the 2020-21 Nets at 211% of their entitlement, beat theirs. What moves is unevenness: the gap between a group’s biggest and smallest ball-holder reads r = −0.65, CI −0.90 to +0.05. The Sixers are at the 90th percentile for combined demand and the 60th for unevenness — extreme on the descriptor that does nothing, ordinary on the one that does.
  8. My own first answer failed its own test. Version one concluded from arithmetic that “the answer is staggering minutes.” Measuring shared floor time for every comparable team since 1999 put that at r = −0.20, CI −0.53 to +0.11 across 23 teams. Not significant, and doubling the history from 13 seasons to 27 moved the sample only from 16 teams to 23 — rosters like this arrive about once a year, so the ceiling is structural, not a data problem.

What I’d do differently

The first version’s headline was a conclusion I had not tested. I derived an overhang from usage arithmetic and asserted the remedy was staggering minutes; when I finally built the test, it came back null. The arithmetic was right and the inference on top of it was not, which is a worse failure than a wrong number because it reads as a finding.

The most useful thing I learned was that reliability and validity are separate tests and a metric has to pass both. Comparing these teams defensively produced r = −0.55 with a bootstrap interval excluding zero — and it was noise: the underlying figure correlates only +0.18 with itself year to year, and on a metric that repeats properly the same quantity is +0.01. The replacement failed the other way. DBPM is +0.78 reliable, but against the matchup metric across 2,203 shared player-seasons it correlates +0.09 with the wrong sign and ranks Embiid last of the five where five other methods rank him first — rock-steady, and measuring activity rather than defence. A wobbly metric is easy to catch because the number moves; a steady wrong one looks exactly like a steady right one. Worth adding that I had told myself no defensive metric spanned that window at all: it was in my own cached files, one column across from the one I was already reading.

I still report a lot of nominal-95% intervals without correction. In the staggering table exactly one of five predictors excludes zero — which is roughly what chance produces at 95%, so it is flagged rather than promoted, but the underlying habit is the thing to fix.

The same denominator was wrong twice, and both times the number looked reasonable. First I summed per-game player averages across a roster and got 36.8 possession-minutes; the tell was team minutes coming to 382 instead of 240. Then, having fixed that, I compared five players against a whole team’s total — which quietly assumes those five play all 240 player-minutes. The published figure moved from 112% to 154%. The habit that fixed it also caught a bug that was not mine: reconciling parts against totals showed the NBA’s own team endpoint reporting 1.69× the sum of its own player rows for 2024-25, a figure that would put one team in possession for 35 minutes of a 48-minute game. The same check passes at 1.003 and 1.005 in the neighbouring seasons, so I rebuild that denominator from player rows rather than trust a total that will not reconcile.

The same source produced a second physically impossible file, and it would have reversed a conclusion. The 2024-25 playoff tracking is inflated about 1.78× — a maximum of 17.1 possession-minutes per 36, which is holding the ball for 47% of your floor time, against a 9.7 high anywhere else in thirteen years. Including it flips the playoff concentration result from “the ball does not concentrate” to “it does”. I caught it because a playoff game is 48 minutes like any other, so the ratio should sit near 1.0 and twelve of thirteen seasons do. It is now excluded by a gate rather than by hand, so the next bad season is caught without anyone remembering to look.

One failure was in the gaps rather than the numbers. The ball comparison first said a bigger overhang went with better results, which is absurd on its face. The players missing were Durant, Kawhi, Zion, Butler and Wall — absent for having missed the preceding season injured — plus two lost to diacritics and one to a suffix. Not random: it strips the biggest ball-holder from exactly the rosters whose stars were fragile, so the gaps were generating the result. Fixing the matching and looking back three seasons moved it from +0.43 to +0.04.

Three further bugs were caught by checks rather than by errors, and all three were silent. A wrong player ID returned a redirect large enough to pass a size-only cache check, so a co-star vanished from the model with nothing raised; the fetcher now verifies page content and the loader refuses to continue on a zero-row parse. Three-pointers were scored as two points in eleven of thirteen seasons because the column carrying shot value only exists from 2023-24 onward. And I compared a residualised regular-season number against a raw playoff one, which reversed a conclusion until both were put on the same basis. The general lesson in all three: the failure mode that costs you is the one that produces a plausible number instead of an exception.

Will he play tonight? — an availability model

~/nba-availability-model · pure standard-library Python

Why I built it
To answer the criticism of my own article. Six weeks after publishing the Bridges piece, I went back and named the four things the LSTM got wrong — it gave a never-injured player near-zero risk, treated each game as independent when absences arrive in multi-week blocks, never compounded per-game risk into the season-long question actually being asked, and had no real out-of-sample test. This rebuilds it to fix all four.
Method
A two-state Markov model: available → unavailable at a per-game onset hazard, then unavailable for a duration drawn from that player’s own observed absence lengths. The hazard is partially pooled toward a league prior with empirical Bayes (method-of-moments Beta, α = 2.71, β = 29.80), which is what stops a one-absence player from being assigned impossible durability. Each player’s season is then simulated 40,000 times. Two parameters per player, no covariates — rest days are computed and displayed but deliberately kept out of the fit.
Validation
A genuine temporal holdout: fit on 2021–22 through 2024–25, tested on 2025–26, 1,311 held-out player-games, scored against deliberately dumb baselines on Brier score and log loss, with a reliability table and interval coverage.
What I found
  1. Mikal Bridges missed 1 of 325 at-risk games in training. Pooled, that is a 1.04% per-game hazard — which compounded across 82 games makes a perfect season a 41.2% proposition. The streak was closer to a coin flip than to a certainty.
  2. Clustering carries almost all the short-horizon signal: one-step-ahead Brier 0.136 against 0.268 for the same player’s marginal rate. Knowing whether he played last night matters more than knowing who he is.
  3. Preseason, player identity carries almost nothing — the pooled model (0.268) barely separates from a flat league base rate (0.270). Worth reporting precisely because it is the unflattering result.
  4. The 90% predictive intervals only covered 68.8% of realised season totals. They are too narrow, and the write-up says so.
What I’d do differently

Absence durations are redrawn independently at each onset, so the model returns injured players too soon — that shows up plainly as the worst-calibrated bin in the reliability table. The 16-player cohort was hand-picked to span durable and fragile archetypes, which means the pooling prior describes that cohort rather than the league. And the simulation propagates season-to-season randomness but not uncertainty in the fitted parameters, which is most of why the intervals are too narrow.
