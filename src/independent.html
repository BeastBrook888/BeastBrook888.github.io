Can five ball-dominant stars share a floor — and who defends when they do?

~/nba_star_fit · numpy and pandas only; the estimators are written out

The question
Philadelphia’s 2026-27 roster puts Tyrese Maxey, Joel Embiid, LeBron James, Jaylen Brown and rookie VJ Edgecombe on one team. A lineup has a finite amount of ball to go round and five men who have each built a career on holding it. Whose share gives — and once you have answered that, who guards anybody?
Method
Four independent readings, because no single one is trustworthy. Within-player usage elasticities from co-star absences, with season fixed effects, hand-computed HC1 errors and a 4,000-replication bootstrap; a two-stage least-squares pass instruments usage with those absences to get the efficiency-versus-usage slope. Matchup-level defence against leave-one-out baselines, residualised for shot mix after the raw version turned out to correlate −0.73 with the share of threes a defender faces. On-court lineups rebuilt from play-by-play substitutions for 27 seasons — validated against independent box scores to 0.43 minutes per game on average, 0.75 at worst — feeding a ridge-regularised adjusted plus-minus and a proper on/off that can hold a teammate fixed. And tracking data for what usage was only ever a proxy for. No statsmodels, no scikit-learn: the normal equations, the robust sandwich, the ridge and the bootstrap are all in the source.
Data
27 seasons of play-by-play rebuilt into 1.3 million lineup stints; 2.0 million matchup rows across nine seasons plus nine more of playoffs; 8,185 player-games from Basketball-Reference; and 69 tracking and play-type files. Roughly 3 GB, cached locally so every figure re-runs offline.
What I found
  1. The measurement I spent the project without turned the headline around. Usage says Embiid is the second-largest claim on the ball. Time of possession says he is the smallest of the five — 3.8 minutes a game at 3.38 seconds per touch, against a league average of 3.37. He runs no pick-and-roll as ball-handler and 22% post-ups, and a post-up is a short possession. Enormous usage, minimal ball-holding.
  2. So the crowding is real but lands elsewhere. A team gets about 24.1 minutes of possession a game; these five have been using 26.9. The player who has to give something up is Maxey (8.4 minutes, 5.79 seconds per touch), not Embiid.
  3. Embiid is the best defender of the five on four independent methods — matchup data, Basketball-Reference on/off, my own segment on/off, and a pooled adjusted plus-minus — and he is the only one who improves in the playoffs (−2.41 points of opponent shooting against his regular-season mark, on a league-wide shift of −0.06).
  4. Maxey and Edgecombe want the same shots: 0.989 cosine similarity across 16 court zones, more alike than 99% of league pairs. The backcourt of the future is the sharpest fit problem in the shot data.
  5. My own first answer failed its own test. Version one concluded from arithmetic that “the answer is staggering minutes.” Measuring shared floor time for every comparable team since 1999 put that at r = −0.20, 95% CI −0.53 to +0.11 across 23 teams. Not significant, and doubling the history from 13 seasons to 27 moved the sample only from 16 teams to 23 — rosters like this arrive about once a year, so the ceiling is structural, not a data problem.

What I’d do differently

The first version’s headline was a conclusion I had not tested. I derived an overhang from usage arithmetic and asserted the remedy was staggering minutes; when I finally built the test, it came back null. The arithmetic was right and the inference on top of it was not, which is a worse failure than a wrong number because it reads as a finding.

I still report a lot of nominal-95% intervals without correction. In the staggering table exactly one of five predictors excludes zero — which is roughly what chance produces at 95%, so it is flagged rather than promoted, but the underlying habit is the thing to fix.

Three bugs were caught by checks rather than by errors, and all three were silent. A wrong player ID returned a redirect large enough to pass a size-only cache check, so a co-star vanished from the model with nothing raised; the fetcher now verifies page content and the loader refuses to continue on a zero-row parse. Three-pointers were scored as two points in eleven of thirteen seasons because the column carrying shot value only exists from 2023-24 onward. And I compared a residualised regular-season number against a raw playoff one, which reversed a conclusion until both were put on the same basis. The general lesson in all three: the failure mode that costs you is the one that produces a plausible number instead of an exception.

Will he play tonight? — an availability model

~/nba-availability-model · pure standard-library Python

Why I built it
To answer the criticism of my own article. Six weeks after publishing the Bridges piece, I went back and named the four things the LSTM got wrong — it gave a never-injured player near-zero risk, treated each game as independent when absences arrive in multi-week blocks, never compounded per-game risk into the season-long question actually being asked, and had no real out-of-sample test. This rebuilds it to fix all four.
Method
A two-state Markov model: available → unavailable at a per-game onset hazard, then unavailable for a duration drawn from that player’s own observed absence lengths. The hazard is partially pooled toward a league prior with empirical Bayes (method-of-moments Beta, α = 2.71, β = 29.80), which is what stops a one-absence player from being assigned impossible durability. Each player’s season is then simulated 40,000 times. Two parameters per player, no covariates — rest days are computed and displayed but deliberately kept out of the fit.
Validation
A genuine temporal holdout: fit on 2021–22 through 2024–25, tested on 2025–26, 1,311 held-out player-games, scored against deliberately dumb baselines on Brier score and log loss, with a reliability table and interval coverage.
What I found
  1. Mikal Bridges missed 1 of 325 at-risk games in training. Pooled, that is a 1.04% per-game hazard — which compounded across 82 games makes a perfect season a 41.2% proposition. The streak was closer to a coin flip than to a certainty.
  2. Clustering carries almost all the short-horizon signal: one-step-ahead Brier 0.136 against 0.268 for the same player’s marginal rate. Knowing whether he played last night matters more than knowing who he is.
  3. Preseason, player identity carries almost nothing — the pooled model (0.268) barely separates from a flat league base rate (0.270). Worth reporting precisely because it is the unflattering result.
  4. The 90% predictive intervals only covered 68.8% of realised season totals. They are too narrow, and the write-up says so.
What I’d do differently

Absence durations are redrawn independently at each onset, so the model returns injured players too soon — that shows up plainly as the worst-calibrated bin in the reliability table. The 16-player cohort was hand-picked to span durable and fragile archetypes, which means the pooling prior describes that cohort rather than the league. And the simulation propagates season-to-season randomness but not uncertainty in the fitted parameters, which is most of why the intervals are too narrow.
