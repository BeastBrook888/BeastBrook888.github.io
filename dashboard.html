Matchup, lineup and tracking data · 2017-18 → 2025-26

Who actually gets hunted on the projected Sixers?

The hypothesis was that Embiid and Jaylen Brown are the solid defenders while Maxey and LeBron are liabilities. Nine seasons of NBA matchup data say that's right about three of five — and that the man opponents should attack is LeBron, not Maxey. Three independent methods were run against each other. They first disagreed completely — then pooling five seasons of rebuilt lineups showed most of that disagreement was noise, and all three now agree on who the best defender is.

A third part compares the roster against the 25 teams since 2000 that assembled three-plus high-usage players. On the ball that comparison works and says something specific: combined appetite for the ball predicts nothing, unevenness is what moves, and the Sixers are extreme on the former and ordinary on the latter. On defence it does not work, for a reason worth reading — the metric that spans the era is reliable and measures the wrong thing.

Late in the project the tracking endpoint opened, and the second half of the page is what that changed. Usage rate — the proxy every earlier conclusion rested on — had the wrong man at the centre of it. Measured by how long he actually holds the ball, Embiid is last of the five, not second, in each of the last three seasons.

✓
Premise confirmed 5 September 2026. All five — Maxey, Embiid, LeBron, Jaylen Brown and VJ Edgecombe — appear on Basketball-Reference's 2026-27 Philadelphia roster. Every earlier version of this page carried this as an unverified assumption; it is now checked. The roster also brings Dean Wade, who is 5th in the league by pooled dRAPM, and Anfernee Simons, a sixth high-usage guard who is a defensive negative.

Method

Why the naive version of this is wrong

Centres get charged their man's blended FG% while actually facing rim attempts, so the untreated metric ranked small guards best and every good centre worst — it was measuring position, not defence. With a single blended baseline it correlated −0.73 with the share of threes a defender faces. Splitting 2s from 3s cut that to −0.36; only residualising on top got it to −0.01. The chart below plots those last two stages. Each confound was found by a face-validity check, not assumed.

Consistency is the whole argument

Single-season intervals are wide, so no one year proves anything. What does carry weight: Embiid is below the zero line in all nine seasons and LeBron is above it in all nine, across three different teams.

Burden vs quality

Edgecombe was never tested

Assignment burden says how featured the men a defender guarded were — it measures the difficulty of the job, not how well he did it. Brown carried the heaviest load of the five. Edgecombe carried the lightest by a wide margin, which is what makes his average rookie grade the least informative number on this page.

Cross-check

Where the second method disagrees

On/off is reported as a check, not a verdict: it systematically penalises high-minute starters, whose on-court minutes face opposing starters while their bench minutes face benches. Negative means the team defended better with him on the floor.

✓
Agree on Embiid and Brown. Embiid is best by both methods and by a wide margin — negative in all nine matchup seasons and all ten on/off seasons. Neither method makes Brown elite.
≠
Disagree on LeBron and Maxey. Matchup data ranks LeBron worst of the five; on/off ranks him second-best. Maxey is the mirror image. Both left unresolved rather than reconciled.
Lineup exposure

What a hunt target costs

Points per 100 possessions above baseline as an opponent shifts a share of its attacks off the lineup's two best defenders and onto its worst. It prices the reallocation, not the absolute level — and in both scenarios the target is LeBron.

Third method

Rebuilding the lineups changed the answer

On-court five-man units were reconstructed from play-by-play substitutions, then intersected between both teams into segments where both lineups are constant, then fed to a ridge-regularised adjusted plus-minus. Unlike raw on/off, both teams' fives are in the design — which is precisely the starter-vs-bench artifact that made on/off unusable.

✓
The earlier “no consensus” finding was largely single-season noise. Rank agreement went from +0.10 to +0.50 against the matchup metric and from −0.20 to +0.80 against on/off. The one-season dRAPM predicts its own pooled counterpart at only +0.30 — which is the clearest evidence that it was measuring mostly noise, and why it was reported as no verdict rather than as a contradiction at the time.
The offensive question

Does staggering actually work?

The usage study concluded these five cannot fit inside the 100 usage points a lineup has — even at each player's most deferential observed rate they sum to 132.5. That made minute allocation, not ball-sharing, the implied lever. This tests whether that holds historically: among teams that assembled three-plus high-usage players, did the ones who kept them apart do better?

⚠
This is underpowered, and more history barely helps. Doubling the window from 13 seasons to 27 moved the comp count only from 16 to 23. Teams that newly assemble three high-usage players are structurally rare — roughly one a year — so this test is limited by how the NBA works, not by how much data was collected. The overlap estimate also moves with sample size (−0.16 → −0.11 → −0.20), which is what an underpowered estimate looks like. It rules out a large, obvious staggering effect; it cannot settle the question at a realistic effect size.
ℹ
The 2017-18 Rockets remain a real existence proof that staggering two ball-dominant guards can work. This says it is not a general law.
Shot diet

Do they want the same shots?

Usage counts a putback and a 19-second isolation identically, so it cannot answer this. Shot locations can. Each player's attempts are split across 16 court zones and compared pairwise — read against the league distribution of similarity, because “0.95” means nothing on its own.

⚠
Maxey and Edgecombe are the sharpest fit problem in the shot data. At 0.989 they are more alike than 99% of league pairs — the backcourt meant to be the future wants the same shots from the same places. Embiid is the opposite: the least similar to everyone, and the least self-creating. He is the one who fits alongside rather than instead of.
What the lineup concedes

The drop-coverage question, finally tested

Every shot is assigned to the segment it falls in, which names both fives — so 638,659 shots are attributable to the five defending them. Early in this project I predicted Embiid's drop coverage plus weak point-of-attack guards would leak pull-up threes and floaters, and framed that as a risk.

✓
The mechanism is real; my reading of it was wrong. Embiid does push shots off the rim into mid-range and floaters — the drop-coverage signature. But that is the coverage working: those are the two least efficient zones in basketball, three-point rate falls 2.3 points and opponent FG% falls 0.9. The pull-up three I warned about barely moves. The real exposure is the reverse — rim attempts climb to 33.6% when he sits.

Two more methods

On/off done properly, and the playoffs

Two tests the earlier work left open. On/off computed from the rebuilt segments can do something Basketball-Reference cannot — hold a teammate fixed, so the comparison is the same guard with and without Embiid rather than starters against bench. And nine playoff seasons ask whether any of this survives in the games the roster was actually assembled to win.

✓
Embiid gets better in the playoffs, not worse. His shift of −2.41 is the largest favourable move of the five, against a league-wide shift of −0.06. LeBron (+1.93) and Brown (+1.14) both go the other way — and Brown's is on the largest sample of the five, 125 games. This is now the fourth method agreeing that Embiid is the best defender in the group.
⚠
One correction worth stating. The first version of this comparison put the playoff figure (raw) against the regular-season figure (residualised for shot mix) and concluded Embiid degrades in the playoffs. Putting both on a raw basis reversed it. Only the shift is interpretable here — the levels still carry the positional bias, which cancels in the difference.
A fourth dataset · new

Rim defence, measured independently

Player tracking counts every shot at the rim and who was defending it. This shares no code and no input data with the matchup work above, so it is a genuine second opinion rather than a restatement. Horizontal is how many rim attempts a player defends per 36 minutes — that is exposure, not skill. Vertical is how many of them drop; lower is better.

✓
The most reliable shooting-defence metric in the project. Rim FG% allowed correlates +0.72 year to year, against +0.50 for the matchup metric this study treats as primary and +0.10 for the perimeter version that had to be dropped. Rim defence is simply the most measurable part of defence: close to the basket the defender's identity is unambiguous, and out on the perimeter it is not. Not exactly like-for-like — the matchup figures span nine seasons and different gates — so read the ordering, not the decimals.

Contest is not deterrence. This counts shots taken against a defender. A rim protector who frightens everyone into passing out records few attempts — and so does a guard nobody bothers to post up. Volume alone cannot separate them. Deterrence is measured instead by what happens to opponent attempt rates when a player is on the floor, which is the drop-coverage section above: Embiid's presence moves rim attempts down 2.0 points into mid-range and floaters, with three-point rate down 2.3 and opponent FG% down 0.9. The two methods agree, and only Embiid clears both.

Part two · offence

Usage was the wrong measurement

Every conclusion this project drew about ball-dominance used usage rate, which counts a putback and a nineteen-second isolation identically. That was flagged as the central limitation from the first day and could not be fixed until the tracking endpoint opened. Time of possession is the honest version: how many minutes a player actually holds the ball. Measuring it moved Embiid from second of the five to last.

⚠
This reversed the study's central attribution. Embiid finishes a large share of possessions without holding the ball long — he runs no pick-and-roll as ball-handler and 22% post-ups, and a post-up is a short possession that ends in a shot. The player who has to give something up is Maxey, not Embiid.
✓
It is not a one-season artifact. Ball-holding correlates +0.93, +0.90 and +0.86 across the three season pairs — about as stable as a basketball statistic gets. Embiid is last of the group in all three seasons and Maxey first in all three, and per-36 normalisation removes the minutes Embiid missed to injury.
Part two · offence

How much ball is there, actually?

Five players at 36 minutes occupy 180 of a game's 240 player-minutes, so they are entitled to 75% of their team's possession time — not all of it. The question is whether these five want more than that.

⚠
The denominator had to be fixed twice. A first attempt summed per-game player averages across a roster and produced 36.8 minutes — wrong, because each average is over that player's own games; the tell was team minutes coming to 382 instead of 240. A second version compared the five against a whole team's total, which assumes they play all 240 player-minutes. The corrected figure is 154%, not the 112% reported earlier.

A proportional cut pushes every one of the five to roughly a league-average ball-holding role — which is the real cost of the fit, and the reason proportional sharing is probably the wrong answer. Note also that the overhang is stable across three seasons and three different teams: held to the four players present in all of them, it reads 160%, 162%, 156%. It is a property of these players, not of one roster.

Two data problems found while building this
  • The NBA's team-level endpoint is corrupt for 2024-25. It reports 1.69× the sum of its own player rows — one team in possession for 35 minutes of a 48-minute game, which would put both teams together at about 70. The same reconciliation check passes at 1.003 and 1.005 for the other two seasons, so the player rows are sound and the team row is the broken one. Every denominator here is rebuilt from player rows.
  • There is a league-wide level shift between 2024-25 and 2025-26. Possession time per team rose from 20.8 to 24.1 minutes with 26 of 30 teams up. A 16% move in one year is more likely a change in how a touch is measured than a change in how basketball is played, but that cannot be settled from the data. Consequence: levels are never compared across that boundary — everything shown is a within-season ratio or percentile, both of which survive a level shift because numerator and denominator move together.
Part three · the comparison

Against teams that actually tried this

Everything above describes one roster. The obvious question is whether the shape it forms has worked before — so the comp set is every team since 2000 that put three or more players at 24%+ usage on the floor together with at least two newly acquired. That is 25 team-seasons, and roughly one arrives a year. The outcome measure is wins minus the preseason Vegas total, because a team with three stars ought to win a lot regardless — the question is whether it beat what the market already expected.

Each star's input is measured in the seasons before he arrived. That is what a front office knew when it made the trade, and it keeps the predictor out of the outcome year — measuring ball-dominance or defence in the same season as the wins would be partly circular.

The ball · new

Does wanting too much of the ball actually hurt?

The earlier staggering test compared these teams on usage. Since measuring possession time reversed which Sixer this project thought was ball-dominant, the comp question is re-asked on the honest measure. Ratio is the group's combined prior-season possession time as a share of what its floor time entitles it to — 100% means it wants exactly its share.

✓
Combined appetite predicts nothing. Ratio correlates +0.04 with beating the line. The hungriest group of the tracking era — the 2020-21 Nets at 211%, with Durant, Harden and Irving all arriving off huge workloads — beat its line by 2.5. The Sixers' 154% is the 90th percentile here, and on this evidence that is not the thing that sinks these teams.
⚠
Unevenness is what moves. The gap between a group's biggest and smallest ball-holder correlates −0.65 [−0.90, +0.05] — the strongest offensive-fit signal anywhere in this project, and it still touches zero. The Sixers sit at the 60th percentile: Maxey wants roughly twice Embiid's ball, which is a real gap but an ordinary one by these standards.

A missingness artifact that reversed the answer, and how it was caught
  • The first run of this said +0.43 — a bigger overhang went with better results, which makes no sense. The cause was who was missing: Durant, Kawhi Leonard, Zion Williamson, Jimmy Butler and John Wall were all being dropped for having no tracking row in the immediately preceding season, because they had missed it injured. Two more were lost to diacritics (Dragić, Bogdanović) and one to a suffix (Marcus Morris Sr.).
  • That loss is the opposite of random: it strips the largest ball-holder from exactly the rosters whose stars were fragile, which pushes their ratio down while their results were already bad. The correlation was being manufactured by the gaps.
  • Fixed by normalising names for diacritics and suffixes, looking back up to three seasons for the most recent one a player actually played, and refusing any team without all of its stars measured. The same correlation is now +0.04.
Defence · new

The defensive comparison, and why I do not lean on it

This is the second attempt. The first used single-season defensive on/off from the rebuilt lineups, produced a clean −0.55 with an interval excluding zero, and had to be retracted — the measure is only +0.18 reliable year to year, and +0.03 at team level. A variable that does not correlate with itself cannot honestly correlate with wins.

✓
The retracted result does not survive the better metric. Group defensive level went from −0.55 on the unreliable measure to +0.01 on the reliable one. That is what a spurious correlation looks like once the noise is taken out. Nothing in the redone version clears zero, and the Sixers are not an outlier on any descriptor.
⚠
But DBPM fails a validity check, which reliability cannot rescue. Against this project's matchup metric across 2,203 shared player-seasons it correlates +0.09 with the wrong sign — and wrong-signed inside every position group. It ranks Embiid last of the five where five other methods rank him first. It is built from steals, blocks and rebounds, which reward activity, not the ability to make your man miss. Reliability and validity are separate tests and a metric has to pass both — on/off failed the first, DBPM fails the second.
ComparisonrExpected signVerdict

What it would take. A defensive measure both reliable per player and available back to 2000. Multi-year RAPM is reliable enough but needs several seasons per player, which one-off assembly seasons do not provide. The matchup metric measures the right thing but starts in 2017-18, which reaches about six comp teams. The offensive comparison is answerable; the defensive one is not yet — and that is a fact about the available data rather than a choice.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Tracking data is now in — the endpoint that was blocked for the whole of this project opened on 8 September 2026. Rim contests and time-of-possession are measured in the two sections above. What is still not directly measurable from the matchup data is "his presence discouraged the shot"; that comes from on/off attempt rates instead.
  • Assignments are nominal. Scoring attribution is internally exact (verified to 0.0000), but who was responsible is NBA's attribution, imperfect on switches and zone. A help defender who roams off his man wears his man's open looks.
  • Perimeter shooting defence is effectively unmeasured — dFG3% failed its reliability test (r=+0.10) and was dropped, so nothing here rests on it.
  • Embiid's samples are thin and post-surgery — 38 games in 2025-26, 19 in 2023-24.
  • Edgecombe is one rookie season on the softest assignments of the five.
  • Single-season APM was too shrunk to use — Embiid's estimate was 62% prior, 38% his own play. Pooling five seasons of rebuilt lineups fixed it, and the pooled version is what appears here; the single-season numbers are retained only for the comparison.
  • 2.15% of team-periods could not be resolved and were dropped, so ~4.7% of scoring falls outside a usable segment.
  • Possessions are estimated, not counted — FGA − OREB + TOV + 0.44·FTA, where 0.44 is a league-average constant.
  • Opponent shot-profile allowed rests on raw on/off — it holds the guard fixed and nothing else, so it is not adjusted for who replaces a player or for opponent quality. Direction is informative; magnitudes are approximate.
  • The staggering test rests on 23 teams drawn from 27 seasons, and the star threshold had to be loosened to 24% usage to reach even that. The estimate moved from −0.157 to −0.201 as comps were added, which signals underpowered rather than converged. Treat the null as "no large effect visible", not as "no effect".
  • The defensive comparison against similar teams does not work yet. Its first version was retracted — single-season on/off is +0.18 reliable, so the −0.55 it produced was noise. Redone on DBPM (+0.78 reliable) nothing clears zero, but DBPM correlates +0.09 with the wrong sign against the matchup metric and ranks Embiid last of the five. Reliable, and measuring something other than defence.
  • The ball-dominance comparison rests on 10 teams. Tracking starts in 2013-14, so unlike the staggering test this cannot be rescued by reaching further back — the data does not exist. Its strongest signal (spread, −0.65) has an interval that touches zero.
  • Every comp-set null is a detection limit, not a finding. At 25 teams a true correlation of 0.40 would be caught only 62% of the time.
  • Comp-set inputs are prior-season, outcomes are same-season. That is deliberate — it keeps the predictor out of the outcome year — but it means a star who changed level on arrival is measured at his old one.
  • Pooled RAPM assumes a player's impact is stable across the window. That is least true for LeBron, who ages from 36 to 41 inside it. One coefficient per player is the price of the extra data.
